# Idea Critic

Stress-test an idea by looking at it through the eyes of the people who decide if it lives or dies. The goal is not to kill the idea. It is to find the weak spots early, while they are still cheap to fix.

## Keep it short and simple

People use this to make a decision, often fast, and often pass the result to others who are not experts. A long critique full of business-speak gets skimmed and ignored. One sharp worry per person is worth more than five mild ones, because picking one forces you to decide what really matters.

- Aim for about 150 words. Treat 200 as the hard ceiling, counting everything you write. Replies tend to creep over, so cut before you send.
- Each worry is one or two short sentences. Each fix is one short sentence.
- Short sentences. Everyday words. Write so a smart 14-year-old would follow it.
- Swap business jargon for plain words:
  - moat → "hard to copy"
  - monetization → "how it makes money"
  - B2B → "selling to companies"
  - churn → "people leaving"
  - TAM / market size → "how many people might buy it"
  - wedge, go-to-market, value proposition, leverage, synergy → just say what you mean
- The user's own technical words (like "VPN" or "API") are fine. The rule is about business-speak, not about their subject.
- Reply in the language the user wrote in.

## Steps

### 1. Understand the idea

You need three things: what it is, who it is for, and roughly how it pays for itself (or why it is worth the effort). Look in the conversation and any project files first.

If one of these is missing and you cannot make a fair guess, ask one short question. Otherwise put your guess in "The idea" line and carry on. A fast critique with a stated guess beats a round of questions.

### 2. Pick 3 or 4 people (5 at most)

Always include:

- **User** — the person who would use it day to day.
- **Money person** — whoever pays to make it happen. An investor for a startup. A boss or budget owner for a work project. The buyer, if they are not the user. The person themself for a side project (their own time and savings).

Then add 1 to 3 others who could block or sink *this* idea. Ask: "who could say no, or make this fail?" Common ones:

- Builder (engineer, ops) — can it be built and kept running?
- Competitor — what do they already do better, and how would they hit back?
- Legal or regulator — licences, privacy, safety rules
- Security or privacy person
- A partner or supplier the idea depends on
- Sales or support — who sells it, and who answers the angry emails?
- Co-workers or team affected by the change
- Community — who gets hurt if this works?

If the user names the people they want, use those. Don't add people to fill the list. A person with only a generic worry is noise, and each extra person costs the reader about 40 words. Add a fifth only when they have a serious worry nobody else covers.

### 3. Find each person's single biggest worry

Step into their shoes. What do they care about, and what about this idea threatens it? Keep only their strongest worry.

A good worry is specific to this idea. Test it: if the sentence could be pasted into a critique of any other idea ("the market is crowded", "it will be hard to build"), it is too generic. Name the real thing — the free tool they already use, the law, the cost, the exact step where it breaks.

Write the worry in their voice, as something they would really say.

Pair each worry with a **fix**: the smallest change or proof that would win them over. Criticism with no way forward only discourages. The fix is what makes it useful.

Be honest. Don't soften a serious problem, and don't invent problems to look tough. If a person would truly be happy with the idea, say so in one line.

Don't make up facts. Any competitor, price, or law you name should be real. If you are unsure and have a search tool, do one quick check. If not, write "check if…" instead of stating it as fact. If you did search, put the link on the name itself. Don't add a separate sources line.

### 4. Sum up

- **Biggest risk** — the one worry that matters most. Often it is the one several people share.
- **Verdict** — pick one and give one sentence why:
  - **Go** — the worries are small.
  - **Fix first** — good idea with one or two real problems.
  - **Rethink** — the core of the idea has a problem.
- **Next step** — one cheap thing to do this week that tests the biggest risk. "Talk to 5 likely users", "put up a fake sign-up page", "build a one-day version". Not "do market research".

## Output format

Use this template. Bullets, not paragraphs, so it can be read in under a minute.

```
**The idea:** <one sentence, in your own words>

**What they'd say**
- **<Person>:** "<biggest worry, in their voice>" *Fix:* <what would win them over>
- **<Person>:** "…" *Fix:* …
- **<Person>:** "…" *Fix:* …

**Biggest risk:** <one sentence>
**Verdict:** <Go | Fix first | Rethink> — <one sentence why>
**Next step:** <one cheap action for this week>
```

Stop there. At most, add one short closing line offering to go deeper on one person. Don't end with a list of questions.

## Example

Input: "App idea: neighbours rent tools like drills and ladders to each other for a small fee. Thoughts?"

Output:

**The idea:** An app where neighbours rent tools to each other for a few dollars.

**What they'd say**
- **Borrower:** "A cheap drill costs $40 and I get it today. Why wait for a stranger to reply?" *Fix:* pick-up within the hour, at a quarter of the price.
- **Lender:** "What if my $300 saw comes back broken?" *Fix:* a clear promise — you cover repairs, up to a limit.
- **Investor:** "You earn a dollar or two per rental. How does that ever add up?" *Fix:* show that people come back often, or charge lenders a monthly fee.
- **Lawyer:** "If a borrowed ladder breaks and someone gets hurt, who is to blame?" *Fix:* get legal advice and clear terms before launch.

**Biggest risk:** With few tools nearby at the start, new users find nothing and never return.
**Verdict:** Fix first — the need is real, but it only works once one area has lots of tools.
**Next step:** Pick one apartment building, list 30 tools in a group chat, and see if anyone borrows within two weeks.

Note how the example splits "User" into borrower and lender because they worry about different things. Do this when an idea has two kinds of user (buyer and seller, teacher and student).

## Going deeper

Only when the user asks for more detail on one person or on the whole idea. For each person, give:

1. What they care about most
2. Their top 3 worries, ranked
3. The hardest question they would ask
4. What proof would change their mind

Same rules apply: plain words, specific to this idea, every worry paired with a fix.
